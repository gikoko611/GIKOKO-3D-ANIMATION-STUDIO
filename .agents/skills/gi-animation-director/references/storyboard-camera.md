# Story, Storyboard and Cinematic Camera

## Story structure
1. Hook — show a visual question, surprise or conflict immediately.
2. Setup — establish character, place and goal.
3. Escalation — make the problem harder through visible actions.
4. Payoff — deliver the joke, reveal, win or emotional beat.
5. Button — finish with a memorable final image or short tag.

For episodic work, maintain a series bible with character goals, relationships and visual rules.

## Storyboard shot schema
Every shot specifies scene_id, shot_id, duration, frame range, location, time of day, visible character IDs, action, emotion, key pose, dialogue, speaker ID, subtitle text, shot size, camera angle, lens/projection, camera movement, lighting, effects, sound cue, transition, asset dependencies and render notes.

## Camera language
- Establishing wide: geography and scale.
- Wide/full: body action and staging.
- Medium: interaction and gesture.
- Close-up: emotion, reaction and dialogue.
- Insert: important prop or detail.
- Over-the-shoulder: conversational spatial relation.

For every camera move specify start/end framing, target, duration and easing. Use motivated pans, tilts, dolly moves and tracking. Avoid camera movement that competes with character action.

## Continuity
Preserve screen direction, eyelines, prop positions, scene geography, character entrances/exits, camera height, focal length and horizon unless a deliberate change is storyboarded.

## Timing and dialogue
Create a timing sheet before rendering. Allocate frames to anticipation, action, reaction and settle. Store dialogue as structured data with speaker ID, exact text, language, emotion and start/end time. Keep subtitles separate from image generation so they remain editable. For Myanmar text, use a Unicode-compatible font and verify glyph shaping in the final MP4.

## Transitions
Choose cuts, match cuts, wipes, dissolves or graphic transitions based on story intent, not decoration.
